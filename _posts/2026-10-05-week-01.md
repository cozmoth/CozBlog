## Overview
In Week 1, I explored how I could use my experiences to develop an idea. When making games for this course, I have been deliberate about including some form of nature or environment alongside technology. I often see people playing cyberpunk-style shooters that seem to promote our current way of life, which makes me question what path people want to follow and why. 

In recent years, some of the most prevalent games I have encountered have been cyberpunk and other dystopian games. The few non-dystopian games seem to lack the same appeal, perhaps because they have less conflict or are simply simulator games. There is something that draws people to dystopian worlds that feel close to real life. This applies to many forms of media, from the news to the stories we read: the plots are always more interesting, but they rarely offer a truly happy ending or a reason to be hopeful.

One thing I found interesting came up when I spoke with a friend the other week. He told me he had been watching _My Hero Academia_. He often struggles with his mental health, but watching All Might gave him a sense of hope that maybe he could do it, too. The excitement in his voice as he realized there might be more to life than he had thought was inspiring.

That conversation got me thinking about hope and how rarely it appears in media today. Even in _My Hero Academia_, All Might represents hope a symbol of peace. But as soon as he is dethroned, everything goes wrong. The people in the story don't know how to create hope for themselves; they rely so heavily on others to provide it that, when they realize no one else can always do so, they turn to despair and sadness. I've seen something similar in real life: people watch videos and say, “This is happening; someone needs to do something about it,” then wait as if someone will miraculously show up to fix everything. If one person eventually acts, others may act too. I think this may be known as the bystander effect. 

### The Bystander effect

To understand the bystander effect more deeply, we must first consider why some people act while others freeze. We often describe responses to unfamiliar situations as “fight or flight.” Someone may fight—whether by attacking or simply trying to talk—while someone else may flee to escape the situation. However, there are two other responses in this framework: fawn and freeze.

Fawn often refers to appeasement behaviors, such as agreeing too readily and monitoring everyone’s emotional state to judge how to respond if things turn sour. Freeze, meanwhile, can involve dissociation or “becoming a statue.”
